A creature made of operators

Each brain region is one switchable part with its own matrix: a map of what follows what, a dopamine-trained reward map per need, drives that gate the readout, a theta clock that separates writing from reading, and grid rings whose weights are generated from two "genes" instead of stored. It explores, gets hungry and thirsty, and finds its way. Open a door it has never walked through and watch what each part does.

Pre-registered tests

Gates and thresholds were committed in PREREG.md before any code ran. 10 seeds each. These show each part doing the job attributed to its region, in a model built to have those parts; they do not show the brain works this way.

What the tests found

The map works up close and fades with distance. After exploring without any reward and tasting food once, the creature walks to it at 1.14× the shortest path, while model-free learning on the same experience wanders (8.3×). But the map's value gradient fades past about 12 steps: the need gate picks the right resource 92.5% of the time when it is near and 19% when it is far.

The grid gives what the map can't. Door C opens in a wall the creature only ever bumped into. With grid rings it walks straight through, at the shortest path, on the first try. The map alone cannot, because it has never experienced that passage.

The theta separation did nothing measurable here. Writing the retrieved read-out back into the map lowered its accuracy by under 0.02 correlation, and behaviour after a door change was the same. That pre-registered kill condition fired: as modelled, the septum is decoration.

The next part this points to is replay. After the tests (not pre-registered): a longer horizon alone did not help (69%). Twice the exploration did (89.7%, far targets 76.5%). The long-range map has to propagate through experience, which is what hippocampal replay does offline during rest.
